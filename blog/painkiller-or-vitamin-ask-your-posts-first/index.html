Painkiller or vitamin? I asked my own posts before picking the next problem.

By Chaminda DelpagodageNovember 24, 2026Picking a problem

On Monday evening I posted one line on X, and it got more replies than most of my longer posts:

building an app is easy.

prioritizing which features to build, is not.

The same is true one level up. Picking which problem to build for is the hard part, and it is the part I have been worst at. This week I tried something new before picking: I asked my own posts. This is what they said, what I did next, and how you can run the same check tomorrow.

Painkiller vs vitamin, in one paragraph

It is an old startup test. A painkiller fixes something that hurts now: lost money, lost data, a lost night. A vitamin is good for you, someday. People pay for painkillers when it hurts. They mean to buy vitamins, and then do not. It is the same thing as the first of the two questions I ask before I pick a problem worth solving: has anyone paid, in money or hours, to make this go away? Three of my four written postmortems fail it. In this language, those were vitamins.

The trouble is that from the inside, every idea feels like a painkiller. I needed an outside read.

What my own posts said

I built NicheSlap to count my niche changes on X, and it also reads each post on its own and groups them into content pillars: what each post is actually about, not what my bio says. Inside my current niche, my public report on November 24 ranked my pillars against my normal post (119 median views):

  • Agent and security rules: 158 median views, 1.3 times my normal, 19 posts, 4 replies a post.
  • Small app build logs: 148, 7 posts.
  • Niche-flapping and NicheSlap: 137, 9 posts.
  • Staying with one problem: 132, 10 posts.

At the bottom: second-chapter checklists and prompts, 12 posts at 93 median views.

Read that list as painkiller vs vitamin and it is hard to miss. My top pillar is all pain. On November 20 I posted:

"stripe handles payments, so payments are safe" is how builders end up giving away paid plans.

your webhook url is public. if you don't verify the signature, anyone can send a fake "payment succeeded" event.

check the signature first. then touch the database.

And this morning:

"i'll add security once i have users" gets the order backwards.

your first real user is also the first real data you can leak. and launch week is when you have the least time to fix anything.

security you add later is security you add during an incident.

Those posts are about something that costs a builder money or data on a specific day. The checklists at the bottom are good for you, someday. My readers reacted the way buyers usually do: more to the pain than to the vitamin.

It is also the answer to the second question. Two decades in automation and security left me knowing a lot of boring pain, which is what I wrote on November 19. My posts now say that is the pain my readers respond to as well.

Attention is not payment, so I asked

Views on a pillar tell you which pain your readers feel. They do not tell you anyone will pay. So on Monday morning I asked the painkiller question out loud on X:

what stage of founders you think are willing to pay a few hundred bucks for a security and reliability review of their product?

i'd review things like (not limited to):

- API keys exposed in web or app
- DNS and domain locked down
- failover and uptime alerts
- key rotation
- who has admin access
- AI spend caps
- Agent workflows and permissions

if yes, what would do you think will make it worth it?

As I write this it has 90 views and one reply. That is not a yes. After one day it is not a no either. What matters is that the question is now public, specific, and has a price range in it, so the answer, when it comes, will mean something. A vague "would you use this?" gets polite yeses. "Would you pay a few hundred bucks for this list?" does not.

When it is a vitamin, design for it

Not everything I build has to be a painkiller. Own Island, the Buddha quotes app I am building, is a vitamin by this test: nobody loses money if they skip a quote today. On Monday I posted about the one design decision that follows from that:

it had put a limit on the number of quotes available to free users (60) vs paid users getting all 600 quotes.

this is a bad split. free users can end up seeing the same quotes rotating thinking it's a product limitation and leave even before they feel the need for the paid features.

so i opened up all 600 quotes for free users.

A painkiller can charge at the moment it hurts. A vitamin has to be felt first, so the free part has to be generous enough to build the habit. Knowing which one you are building changes where the paywall goes.

This is narrowing, not a pivot

Reading my pillars could easily have turned into another hop: security looks good, drop everything, start a security business. I have done that kind of switch three times on X in seven months. This time the top pillar serves the same reader I already write for, builders shipping their own products, so it is a narrower problem inside the niche I am in, not a new one. Fewer bets, longer stays still applies. The pillar just tells me which bet to place next.

How to run the check on your own posts

  1. Group your last 30 to 60 posts by what each one is about. Not by what your bio says. How to do it by hand is in content pillars on X on the NicheSlap blog.
  2. Find the pillar that beats your normal post on median views and replies, over the same dates.
  3. Write its pain sentence: who loses what, and when, if this is not fixed? If you cannot name money, data, time or a specific bad day, it is a vitamin.
  4. Ask the paying question in public, with a concrete list and a price range, the way I did above. Score your idea against existing spend with the SaaS idea scorecard while you wait.
  5. If it is a vitamin, keep it, but design for it: free part generous enough to build the habit, paywall after the value is felt.

One thing to do tomorrow

Pick your best-performing post from the last month and write one sentence under it: who loses what if this problem is not fixed? If the sentence comes easily, ask your readers whether they would pay to fix it. If it does not, you have found a vitamin, and that is fine, as long as you know it before you build the paywall.

Your own posts are one demand signal. Other people's posts are the other. For watching X for people who describe the problem in their own words, I wrote Twitter keyword alerts for founders: watch for the problem, not your brand name on the ReplyWisely blog.
