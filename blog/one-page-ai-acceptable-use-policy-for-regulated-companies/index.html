The one-page AI acceptable use policy for regulated companies.

By Chaminda DelpagodageNovember 28, 2026Advisory

Most AI policies fail for one reason. They are written before anyone knows how the tools get used, and they are too long to read. I wrote about this on X:

stop starting your AI rollout with a 20-page policy. nobody reads it, and it's written before anyone knows how the tools get used. start with one page: approved tools, data that never goes in, who to ask. let the pilot write the rest.
@chams_builds, Nov 23, 2026

This post is the one page. What goes on it, what stays off, and the extra lines a company with PCI, HIPAA or SOC 2 in scope should add. If you would rather start from a draft, the AI Use Policy Starter builds one in your browser.

The eight parts of the page

  1. Approved tools. A short list, by name and plan, used with company accounts.
  2. Data that never goes in. A list people can remember.
  3. What to ask first. The few uses that need a yes before they start.
  4. Who to ask. One named person or channel, and how fast they answer.
  5. You own the output. A person reads anything that reaches a customer.
  6. Exceptions. Written down, with an owner and an end date.
  7. Mistakes. How to report one, with no blame for reporting quickly.
  8. An owner and a review date. Someone who keeps the page alive.

Approved tools: the part that stops shadow AI

A ban does not stop AI use. It moves it somewhere you cannot see. People still need the work done, so they use a personal account and paste in what they have. I put it this way on X:

"no" doesn't reduce risk. it just moves it somewhere you can't see.
@chams_builds, Nov 25, 2026

An approved tool is the only kind security can watch. So the first line of the page is a yes, with a list: these tools, on company accounts. Say plainly that personal accounts are not for company work, even for the tools on the list.

Data that never goes in

Keep this list short enough to remember. Passwords and keys. Customer and employee personal information. Anything a contract says you cannot share. Then add the lines your frameworks need:

  • PCI DSS in scope: payment card numbers and other cardholder data never go into an AI tool.
  • HIPAA in scope: health information stays out unless a tool has been approved for it in writing.
  • SOC 2 in scope: tool approvals, exceptions and reviews are recorded, so you can show what was decided and when.

The same idea works for requests. In The Data You Never Needed, I wrote that a "no" from security is usually a no to one sentence, "give AI access to our data", because it does not say which data. Ask people for four plain answers: the job, the fields it needs, where the data will sit, and who can open it. Put those four questions in the policy as the way to ask for a new tool.

Yes, with boundaries

The page works best when each rule starts with a yes. These are the four I wrote down on X:

  • yes, but only approved tools, with SSO and logging.
  • yes, but no customer or regulated data unless the contract covers it.
  • yes, but agents get the least access that works, not a shared admin token.
  • yes, but a human owns every AI decision that touches money, access, or customers.

That is a boring list on purpose. It lets people move without betting the company.

Exceptions need an end date

Every policy gets exceptions. The risk is that a temporary yes becomes permanent access, long after the pilot that justified it has ended. I covered this in The Pilot Ended. The Exception Didn't. One line in the policy handles most of it: an exception is written down with a reason, an owner, the control that reduces the risk, and an end date.

Name an owner

The last line decides whether the page lives. I have said it this way:

"we're not ready for AI" usually means nobody owns the decision.
@chams_builds, Nov 24, 2026

Put one named owner on the page, and a review date. Models change, vendors change their terms, and usage creeps. The policy has to keep up.

What to leave out

Leave the long parts for later: a full vendor-by-vendor risk list, ethics statements, and detailed technical standards. They can follow once a pilot shows what your teams actually do. An auditor will not ask how clever your AI is. They will ask what AI is in use, who approved it, and what data goes into it. One page can answer those.

This is not legal advice. Have your legal, security and compliance leads review any policy before you publish it.
