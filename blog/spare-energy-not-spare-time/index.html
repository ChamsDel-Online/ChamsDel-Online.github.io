Spare energy, not spare time: how I pick what to build with a day job and kids.

By Chaminda DelpagodageNovember 23, 2026Picking a problem

On Friday evening I posted this on X:

"build in your spare time" assumes you have spare time. with a day job and kids, what you have is a little spare energy.

so pick the problem that gives you energy back after a long day, not the one that looks best on paper.

which idea would you still open at 9pm?

This morning I posted the other half, the part about what I actually did with that spare energy this year. This post puts the two together: why I now pick what to build by energy instead of by hours, and a one-line test you can run tonight on the last project you dropped.

The hours were never the scarce part

Every plan for building on the side starts by finding time. I found it a long time ago: I build at 5am and in the margins of weekends. Those hours show up every week. What does not always show up is the energy to use them well after a full day of work and bedtime with a young family.

That changes what "a good idea" means. An idea that looks great on paper but drains me will lose to sleep every time. An idea that is a little smaller but gives me energy back gets opened, and an idea that gets opened is the only kind that ever gets far enough to give an answer.

In the early hours of that same Friday I put the stakes more bluntly on X:

building something on the margins should be a way of self expression.

if not you’re just grinding for the second time.

I already have a job. If the side project feels like a second one, the margins are the first thing I give back.

New-repo energy is not problem energy

Here is what I posted this morning:

"just keep shipping" quietly turns into just keep starting.

i created 33 repos in the last 12 months. a new repo feels like progress. it’s the cheapest part of building.

before your next git init, reopen the last project you dropped. write one line on why you left.

Starting gives off its own energy: a blank repo, a new name, a domain, a first screen that works. It is real, and it has almost nothing to do with the problem. It runs out when the work stops being new, and that happens to every idea, good or bad. If an idea only gave me energy in its first week, it was the repo giving me energy, not the problem.

Problem energy feels different. It is the idea I keep thinking about when I am not working on it. My clearest examples are the ones where I was the user. I built Steady for a problem I had that morning. I built NicheSlap because I had changed my niche on X three times in seven months and could not see it until something counted my posts. Both started from a problem I had, not from a list of ideas.

Just after midnight last night I posted the honest scoreboard on X:

i didn’t make any real money from building for more than a year now.

but i found what makes me really tick: building and creating.

Both lines are true. The second one is why I keep building. The first one is why I can no longer afford to spend the energy on starting things I will not stay with.

Age is not the constraint either

The other excuse I hear is age. On Saturday I posted:

"you’re too late to start at 43" gets it backwards.

twenty years in, you know which problems are expensive, who pays for them and what breaks at scale. a 22-year-old has to guess all of that.

stop counting the years you lost. list the problems you’ve already seen up close.

Experience helps the energy problem too. A problem I have seen up close does not need to be researched from scratch at 9pm. I already know the people, the workaround they use today, and why the obvious fix fails. That is the second of the two questions I ask before I pick a problem worth solving.

The one-line test for what you already dropped

Before the next git init, reopen the last project you dropped and write one line on why you left. Then label the line with one of two letters:

  • M, the market said no. You asked, and the answer was clear.
  • E, the energy ran out. Nobody said no. You stopped opening it.

Four of my shut-down products have written postmortems, so their lines are easy:

  • CampfireX: nobody paid in eight days. M.
  • DearMeWiser: the free fix was one prompt away. M.
  • CompliQuiz: I built the step nobody pays for. M.
  • HardChoice: it worked, but it needed an audience I did not have. M, mostly.

Those are the tidy ones. Most of the other repos never got a postmortem, and when I am honest about them, the line is closer to what I wrote on November 17 in the small bets post: I did not want to get too close to any one idea. That is an E. No better idea fixes an E. Picking by energy might.

How I pick now: the 9pm test

  1. List problems, not ideas. Problems you have, or have seen up close. An idea is one possible fix. You can swap the fix later without losing the problem.
  2. Picture the next session at 9pm after a long day. What is the very next task for each one? If the honest answer is setup (a repo, a domain, a logo), that is new-repo energy. If it is a real step for a real user, keep it on the list.
  3. Check which one you thought about this week without being asked. On a bad day, in the car, in the shower. That one is giving energy back.
  4. Read your M and E lines. If most of them are E, pick the problem closest to your own life, even if it looks smaller on paper.
  5. Pick one, and write its "why I would leave" line in advance. The result that would make it an M, and the date you will check. The rest of the method is in six rules for fewer bets and longer stays.

One thing to do tomorrow

Do not start anything. Reopen the last project you dropped, write one line on why you left, and mark it M or E. If you have time for one more, do the next one. Three lines in, you will know whether you need a better idea or a better reason to stay.

The same test on X

I ran the same "why did I leave" exercise on my own X niches. The count said three niches in seven months, about 107 days each. I wrote up how to do it for your own account as an X account audit on the NicheSlap blog.

If you are earlier than this

If you do not have a problem on the list yet, start one step back. I built IkigaiNiche as a free guided way to find a person you would be glad to help and a problem of theirs worth solving. Once you have one and you post about it on X, NicheSlap is the next step: it stress-tests whether you are actually staying with it.
